# Fix the write path before tuning connections

Rays's connection-scaling logic already matches the best published practice, so the next gains come from the machinery around it, not from smarter algorithms. The engine starts files of 64 MiB or more on 4 parts, grows connections one step at a time with a thr/1.02^n utility, halves the largest unfinished segment when a connection frees up, and honors `Retry-After`. That is the same utility shape and K = 1.02 that Falcon, Marlin, AutoMDT, EDT and FastBioDL all converged on ([FastBioDL](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.05511), [AutoMDT](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.05546v1)). What remains open is mostly the receive-to-disk path and the tail of the download. The single most likely bottleneck is the per-chunk `write_all` plus `flush` on `tokio::fs`: every call is a `spawn_blocking` hop, so a 1.25 GB/s stream of 16 KB TLS records means roughly 76k chunks and about 150k thread handoffs per second ([tokio::fs docs](https://docs.rs/tokio/latest/tokio/fs/index.html)) [U on magnitude, no measured per-hop cost exists]. After that come hedged tail requests, a relative-speed slow-connection rule, a per-host learned connection ceiling, and cheap config items (SQLite `synchronous`, release `opt-level`, `F_PREALLOCATE`). Everything is a hypothesis until measured on a fixed A/B benchmark, because the research found no published number for Rays's exact stack. The ranked action list and a "what not to do" list close the report.

## How Rays downloads today, and where it already matches the literature

The engine probes the URL for size and range support, then plans segments. Files of 64 MiB or more begin with 4 parts and a single warm tick; fresh splits reuse the probe's own connection for part 0, which removes the wasted handshake the earlier audit flagged. A 1 s TICK sampler measures aggregate speed over WINDOW_TICKS = 2 after WARM_TICKS = 1, adds a connection when throughput improves, and steps down when speed/1.02^n does not improve (PENALTY, download.rs:323-324). HOLD_TICKS = 15 stops it from oscillating. The ceiling is MAX_CONNECTIONS = 16. When a connection finishes, `split_largest` halves the biggest unfinished segment (download.rs:781), subject to MIN_SPLIT 4 MiB, MIN_SEGMENT 1 MiB and SPLIT_ALIGN 64 KiB. A dead connection is dropped after STALL = 30 s, and retries wait [2, 5, 15, 30, 60] s with `Retry-After` honored up to 2 min.

Against the literature this is well placed. The recurring finding across online concurrency tuners is a thr/K^n utility with K just above 1: FastBioDL's sweep gave 701 Mbps at k = 1.01, **816 Mbps at k = 1.02**, and 744 Mbps at k = 1.05, though on a single dataset and link ([FastBioDL](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.05511)). The same work found gradient descent beat Bayesian optimization by about 20% on total copy time, because BO needs random seed trials and early samples during transients skew its surrogate. Halving the largest remaining range is the IDM dynamic-segmentation idea and the work-stealing scheme in aria2 ([IDM segmentation](https://cn.internetdownloadmanager.com/support/segmentation.html), [aria2 manual](https://aria2.github.io/manual/en/html/aria2c.html)). MIN_SPLIT of 4 MiB is more aggressive than aria2's 20M default, and the arithmetic supports it: it is roughly 10 bandwidth-delay products per connection at 100 Mbps aggregate over 8 connections [U, unsourced BDP reasoning; no paper relates minimum split size to BDP]. One caution on the ramp: the EDT paper measured 40 to 46 s for 1-step gradient or Falcon-style search to reach mean throughput on 33 to 45 Gbps WAN links, against 3 s for a trained DRL agent ([EDT](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.06159v3)). Those optima sit at 15 to 20+ streams, while desktop ISP links settle at 3 to 8 (FastBioDL averaged 3.4 to 6.8), so Rays's 3 s step reaches its optimum in far fewer steps [U inference]. The cheap lesson from the RL papers is the action shape, not the learning: Jamil et al. used asymmetric steps of +5, +1, -1, -5 ([Jamil et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.11949)). A +2 early step, then +/-1, would borrow that for free.

## The write path is the biggest unmeasured cost

Tokio's own documentation says every `tokio::fs` operation uses `spawn_blocking` on all platforms (io_uring exists only on Linux under `tokio_unstable`) and recommends batching "into as few spawn_blocking calls as possible" ([tokio::fs docs](https://docs.rs/tokio/latest/tokio/fs/index.html)). Rays does the opposite: `write_all` then `flush` per chunk (download.rs:922-923, plus :308 and :316 on the single-stream path), and a flush after each chunk also defeats any `BufWriter`. Hyper's body frames arrive per TLS record regardless of its read buffer, so chunk size is set by the server's record size, not by Rays ([hyper io.rs](https://docs.rs/crate/hyper/1.6.0/source/src/proto/h1/io.rs)). The fix is mechanical: accumulate 256 KiB to 1 MiB per segment, write once with `std::fs::File` and `FileExt::write_all_at` inside one `spawn_blocking`, and drop the flush. Batching to 1 MiB cuts hops about 64x [U, arithmetic]. Apple's file-system guidance agrees that one large write beats many small ones ([Apple performance tips](https://developer.apple.com/library/ios/documentation/FileManagement/Conceptual/FileSystemProgrammingGuide/PerformanceTips/PerformanceTips.html)), and aria2 defaults to a 16M disk cache for the same reason ([aria2 manual](https://aria2.github.io/manual/en/html/aria2c.html)). One design constraint: `seg.done` must keep meaning "bytes on disk", so `record()` runs only after the batch lands and the pause and cancel paths flush the buffer.

Smaller write-side items follow. The earlier audit measured nothing because Cloudflare rejected the missing User-Agent (since fixed), so no throughput baseline exists; build one first. `PRAGMA synchronous=NORMAL` is absent from store.rs; the earlier audit judged it safe because resume re-checks the `.part` size, and the progress save every 2 s under the job mutex is where a FULL-mode WAL fsync can stall every segment [U, from the audit, not profiled]. Preallocation uses `set_len` only (download.rs:282); `fcntl(F_PREALLOCATE)` with an `ftruncate` fallback is the aria2-style fix, but APFS has returned EINVAL on repeated calls, so tolerate failure ([Bitcoin Core PR 17887](https://mirror.b10c.me/bitcoin-bitcoin/17887/)). The full `sync_all` at finish (download.rs:941) is fine on macOS, since plain fsync only reaches the drive; `F_FULLFSYNC` is far slower ([Michael Tsai](https://mjtsai.com/blog/2022/02/17/apple-ssd-benchmarks-and-f_fullsync)), so the question is whether the finish sync should exist at all for a user-facing file [U]. `F_NOCACHE` only stops page-cache retention and no APFS benchmark exists, so test it behind a flag with 1 MiB aligned writes if at all.

Runtime and build settings come second. Tokio defaults to one worker per core, and public reports show worker count rarely changes throughput ([Tokio discussion 7091](https://github.com/tokio-rs/tokio/discussions/7091)); Rays's `worker_threads(2)` (api.rs:25) is probably fine once file I/O leaves the runtime but is a cheap sweep over 2, 4 and 8. Release `opt-level = "s"` (Cargo.toml:9) may cost 5 to 20% in hyper and tokio code, but that estimate is unsourced [U]. Crypto in aws-lc and ring is hand-written assembly and unaffected. On TLS, rustls with aws-lc-rs ranks first in 14 of 16 placement tests against OpenSSL in the Q1 2026 report ([memorysafety Q1 2026](https://www.memorysafety.org/blog/26q1-rustls-performance/)), but no Apple Silicon numbers exist for Secure Transport. The earlier audit advised keeping native-tls to save binary size; the notes say syscall and wakeup overhead, not AES, is the likely limit, so a loopback A/B of both backends is the honest test. Socket buffers are lower priority: macOS autotunes receive buffers to a 4 MB default cap ([ESnet](https://fasterdata.es.net/host-tuning/osx/)), which limits one stream to about 1.6 Gbps at 20 ms RTT, and 16 connections already clear that. Whether `SO_RCVBUF` disables autotuning on Darwin is unverified (BSD behavior; the notes mark it [U]), so leave it alone.

## Tail latency and slow connections need relative rules, not a 30 s timer

The last segments decide how a fast download ends, and today Rays has only STALL = 30 s, which detects dead connections, not slow ones. The Tail at Scale pattern is the right model: send a duplicate only after the first request outlives roughly the 95th-percentile expectation, which caps extra load near 5% ([Acolyer summary of Dean and Barroso](https://blog.acolyer.org/2015/01/15/the-tail-at-scale/)). In its BigTable benchmark a 10 ms hedge cut p99.9 from 1,800 ms to 74 ms for 2% more requests (same summary; original not read). Blind duplication only pays below about 25 to 50% server utilization ([Vulimiri et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1306.3707)), and a download's cost is duplicate bytes, so the hedge must be small. BitTorrent endgame is the same idea: once every block is requested, idle peers ask for blocks still pending elsewhere and the loser is cancelled ([libtorrent](https://blog.libtorrent.org/2011/11/writing-a-fast-piece-picker/)). The S3 guidance is a direct HTTP analogue: for large requests, track throughput and retry the slowest 5% on a new connection ([S3 design patterns](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/optimizing-performance-design-patterns.html)).

No study measured hedged range requests for file downloads, so the thresholds are tunable guesses [U]. The design the notes support is this: when no unassigned bytes remain and a connection is idle, duplicate the tail of the largest unfinished segment, aligned to SPLIT_ALIGN, capped at MIN_SPLIT bytes per segment, and keep the first finisher; this also needs duplicate-safe writes. Add a rolling per-connection speed monitor so a connection below about a quarter of the median for around 10 s triggers the same hedge rather than a kill, because killing resets slow start and may re-hit a throttled path. aria2 is no model here: its `--lowest-speed-limit` defaults to 0 (off), and its stall check is inactivity only (timeout 60 s), plus a faster-mirror swap at most every 10 s ([aria2 manual](https://aria2.github.io/manual/en/html/aria2c.html), [AbstractCommand.cc](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aria2/aria2/master/src/AbstractCommand.cc)). Chunk sizing by measured throughput (MDTP) beat aria2 by 13.69% on 64 GB over six replicas, but in a Python prototype on multi-server FABRIC testbeds, and it requests each chunk once ([MDTP](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09597v1)). Rays's halve-the-largest approach already shifts work toward faster connections, so treat MDTP as validation, not a target.

## Server politeness sets the real ceiling

More connections help only until a per-IP or per-file limit binds, and that limit is host-specific. Evidence is thin: no major CDN publishes a per-connection cap, and only AWS S3 explicitly recommends parallel range GETs to fill the NIC ([S3 design patterns](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/optimizing-performance-design-patterns.html)). For Cloudflare, Akamai, Fastly, Dropbox, GitHub and SourceForge, the notes found nothing citable, so claim nothing about them. Volunteer mirrors do cap: operators describe per-IP limits of 2 to 6 via fail2ban and `mod_limitipconn`, with one operator's daily hits falling from about 700,000 to under 100,000 ([CentOS mirror list](https://lists.centos.org/pipermail/centos-mirror/2010-January/028327.html)). Metalink/HTTP says a client must not make more than one concurrent request per mirror and should find its parallel limit by watching aggregate throughput as connections are added ([RFC 6249](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6249.html)). That is exactly Rays's marginal-gain rule, which is reassuring. Google Drive is different: it enforces a per-file quota, and a 403 `downloadQuotaExceeded` lasts roughly 24 to 48 hours, so backoff is wasted on it ([rclone forum](https://forum.rclone.org/t/googleapi-error-403-the-download-quota-for-this-file-has-been-exceeded-downloadquotaexceeded/22395)).

Three concrete gaps follow. First, `Retry-After` has two forms, delay-seconds and HTTP-date ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/HTTP/Headers/Retry-After)); Rays parses only seconds, so a date-form reply gets the default backoff instead of the server's value. Second, a 429 or 503 on one connection usually signals a limit that applies to all of them, so the right reaction is to lower that host's ceiling toward 1 to 4 and regrow slowly, not to retry at the same width [U inference]. Third, hosts like Drive should end the job on a quota 403 instead of retrying. Multi-source is the larger feature: the classic result is Rodriguez and Biersack's dynamic parallel access, which pulls blocks from several replicas at once with no server changes and reports "significant speedups" ([EURECOM](https://www.eurecom.fr/en/node/4906577), abstract only; numbers unavailable). Rays's per-URL pool probably already balances blocks dynamically, so Metalink (`Link: rel=duplicate`, `Digest`, `If-Match`) is mostly parsing plus a per-mirror scoreboard. It only pays on hosts that publish mirror lists, so it ranks below the fixes above. Application-level multipath over Wi-Fi plus Ethernet via `IP_BOUND_IF` is conceivable, but MPTCP aggregate needs an Apple entitlement and server support, URLSession cannot set it on macOS, and no macOS aggregation benchmark exists ([mptcp.dev](https://mptcp.dev/macOS.html)). Skip it.

## Protocol and learning experiments the evidence argues against

Staying on HTTP/1.1 x N is the right default for single large files. HTTP/2 multiplexes over one TCP connection, so one lost packet stalls every stream, and its default 65,535-byte window caps throughput below the bandwidth-delay product unless windows are enlarged ([Marx et al.](https://speeder.edm.uhasselt.be/webist/files/h2bestpractices_RobinMarx_WEBIST2017.pdf), [curl bug 1102](https://curl.se/bug/?i=1102)). HTTP/3 in reqwest remains behind `--cfg reqwest_unstable` as of the notes' last confirmation (current 2026 status unconfirmed), and academic measurements show QUIC bulk transfers up to about 45% slower than TCP+TLS+H2 on fast links because of CPU cost ([arXiv 2310.09423](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.09423v2)). L4S on macOS covers URLSession TCP downloads and QUIC only, not BSD sockets, and Apple's own demo claimed latency gains, not throughput ([WWDC23 10004](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10004/)). Learned tuners fail the fit test for a desktop client: AutoMDT needs about 45 minutes of offline simulator training and an estimated 5 to 7 days online; the Jamil PPO work needed about 28 hours per testbed ([AutoMDT](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.05546v1), [Jamil et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.11949)). HARP needs a history of about 21K transfers ([HARP](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1708.03053)). A model shipped blind to unknown ISPs would be worse than a +2 then +/-1 hill climb.

## Conclusion

The research reverses the usual intuition: Rays's adaptive concurrency is the part least in need of work. The literature's best results come from the same one-parameter hill climb Rays runs, and the exotic alternatives (DRL, BO, HTTP/3, multipath) lose on fit, not on merit. The gains left sit in two unglamorous places: how fast bytes leave the socket and reach disk, and how the final seconds of a download are handled. Both are testable in a day with an interleaved A/B bench against a fixed URL set, and both fail gracefully if the hedge or batching is disabled.

The honest limit of this report is evidence quality. Per-hop `spawn_blocking` cost, the benefit of hedged ranges on HTTP origins, optimal worker count on Apple Silicon, rustls versus Secure Transport per core, and CDN per-connection caps have no published numbers. Every impact figure below is a [U] estimate until the bench runs; the first deliverable of any optimization pass should be that bench, with write batch size {16 KiB, 256 KiB, 1 MiB, 4 MiB} and worker count {2, 4, 8} as the sweep axes.

## What not to do

Do not build DRL, Bayesian-optimization or bandit tuning for connection count: training cost is hours to days, gains over hill climbing appear on 10 to 100 Gbps WAN links, and the only client-side head-to-head found GD beating BO. Do not make HTTP/3 or HTTP/2-multiplexed ranges the default; keep HTTP/1.1 x N and make anything else opt-in per host after benchmarking. Do not raise MAX_CONNECTIONS above 16 without per-host learning, since mirror operators cap at 2 to 6 per IP. Do not set `SO_RCVBUF` or ask users for sysctl changes before an A/B shows a gain. Do not pursue MPTCP or interface-bound multipath, and do not move to Network.framework or URLSession for speed, since no benchmark supports a gain and Apple forum reports show regressions ([Apple forums 805044](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/805044)). Do not kill slow connections as a first response, because hedging avoids slow-start resets; and do not retry a quota 403 with backoff.
